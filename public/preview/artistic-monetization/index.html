Monetizable Artistic Consumption

As AI makes producting art cheaper, creatives must shift their focus towards actually capturing value from their creations. Platforms that help those artists earn more from their demand will be extremely consequential (cc @Whop @Lovable)

Dhruv Gupta

Walking through Chinatown I am struck by the fact that, in the same city where Spera has nearly doubled its cost per unit over an year, I can still get a hearty dinner for under $10. Chinatown has managed to build its own micro economy within SF, where any service attainable on the outside has an equivalent on the inside, allowing the community to patronize each other and lower $ outflow, while continuing to keep prices low and attract $ inflow from the rest of the city.

The same economic idea (which we will call an enclave economy) underpins why I'm increasingly less worried about software democratizing the ability to write code. As we undergo a 10-100x increase in code being generated, we undergo a similar (if smaller) increase in B2B SaaS startups being born. These startups, and their more mature predecessors, buy products from each other, while also increasingly providing services to outgroups (Fortune 500 companies, small businesses, etc).

Money entering the software enclave economy through more enterprise contracts and adoption + increased VC spending has outpaced the growing spending on human and physical capital. With the ability to generate code no longer being a bottleneck, we have entered an era of abundance in software where an increase in supply also brings an increase in demand.

Artists have been reluctant to accept that AI will transform the way that their work is produced. Recent discourse around AI for game development has brought the issue top of mind again, with both the disdain that artists feel for generative AI and its ability to build experiences that would take them months in a matter of hours never being more clear.

While enablement and acceptance is a cultural problem, I fear that there is currently no clear economic path forward for atrists either.

Even in this era of abundance, SaaS companies falls well into the enclave economy path for multiple reasons

  1. Software is inherently multi-use and subscription based
  2. Software is relatively unapproachable, regardless of how much easier building + deploying has become
  3. Software, especially for necessary but unsexy applications, is uninteresting for most people
  4. Software, if attempted to all be built internally, would become an unmanatianble sprawl, and most companies know this

No one dreamed of growing up to build an AI search company, and when providers like @ExaAILabs or @p0 showed up, no one ever had to think about that problem again. Quite a lot of people, I imagine, dreamed of developing their own game or producing their own movie or creating their own music, limited only by the time and ability to pursue those goals.

Art has some of the same relationships in that filmmakers needs music, musicians needs cover art, game devs need characters, environments, dialogue, etc. But those purchases depend on someone having the budget to finance the finished work and expecting that the net value any artist brings will be greater than the money saved by relying on Opus/Astra.

100x as much art does not correlate to 100x as much spending on art. Consumers have limited attention, and new art will have to compete with an ever-increasing catalogue of existing work.

To earn a livelihood through their art, an aspiring artist must solve a new type of problem: finding paying customers + the means to sell to them.

They can sell their skills to someone who already has a budget, build an audience willing to buy their work, or find patrons willing to finance its creation. They can build businesses around their art that sell merchandise, stickers, collectibles, classes, or live experiences and in doing so give people more reasons to give them their money than access to their work alone. However, each of these paths requires the artist to solve a littany of other problems: distribution, customer acquisition, fulfillment, etc.

In this world, companies like @Whop that abstract away the complicated parts like managing a company's registration, finances, distribution, audience engagement, etc become incredibly invaluable to creatives, who are empowered to focus on their art and nothing else.

Creatives who prefer to build their own stack instead of leaning on an existing platform will find value in companies like @Lovable. A conversation with @antonosika in March convinced me that his company will begin to frame itself as an AI co-founder for small businesses as an extension of their existing perception as one prompt product builders.

Making your art has become easier but making a living from your art hasn't yet caught up. That gap is an exciting opportunity for startups to help creatives turn interest in their work into paying customers, and those customers into a sustainable business.

If creative abundance can translate to economic opportunity, I personally feel much better about a post-AGI future.
