Are quant firms unethical?

What are quant firms, how do they make money, and is the way they make money moral?

Dhruv Gupta

Market makers/wholesalers like Jane Street, Citadel Securities, SIG, and IMC make money by selling assets like stocks or options for marginally more than they're worth, and buying them for marginally less than they're worth (classic buy low, sell high).

Every time you try to buy Apple on Robinhood, it's not going to the NASDAQ or NYSE; it's going to a wholesaler, who will sell you that AAPL for slightly cheaper than you would've gotten it on an exchange.

Not only do these firms do you the great service of giving you a good deal, they also pay Robinhood (or Charles Schwabb) some fee for letting them trade against you. Why be so generous?

Quants are terrified of "adverse selection." The amount of money they make per trade is near 0; their massive profits come from doing millions of trades on both sides of markets per minute.

However, imagine the case of a sell-off. If someone saddles IMC with millions of dollars of a stock minutes before a bad news story causes the price to collapse, IMC will have a very hard time selling off their position without incurring massive losses.

A single trade against smart money (hedge funds, competitors) can wipe out the profits from hundreds of trades against dumb money (retail investors, institutions like pension funds). Therefore, quant firms are willing to pay a premium to trade against dumb money.

In fact, much of what might be considered "strategies" or "alpha" in market making comes down to understanding whether the trader opposite the firm is smart money or dumb money.

Of course, this is a massive oversimplification; the best firms don't just try to keep a neutral book when they market make. They are willing to hold positions and take on risk and thus need to have an understanding of the fair price of assets, beliefs on how assets will move over short and long time horizons, and novel approaches to trading those assets.

However, if you strip it down to just providing liquidity on exchanges, a heuristic of "does the guy I'm trading against know what he's doing" would tend to make money.

Now that we have some understanding of quants, let's chat about options trading.

I won't dive too deeply into the mechanics, but it is important to understand that options trading is negative sum: for every options trade between trader A and trader B, B will lose as much as A gains, and both will lose some % to the market makers.

This is opposed to investing in stocks, which is positive sum since companies can create net new value, causing their equity to appreciate without any other equity depreciating.

As a consequence of this + the sophistication needed to trade options intelligently, the Indian market regulator found that ~90% of options traders lose money, as opposed to ~5% of stock investors (comparisons over 1-year vs 10-year time horizon and India vs US so take with a grain of salt).

It is concerning, therefore, that ~45% of options volume in the US is retail, and that the options markets have been growing at a quicker pace than the stock markets.

Your opinion on whether or not quant firms are a net negative will come down to the question: is it ethical to profit off of making a bad thing marginally less bad?

Compare it to the cigarette industry. If someone made tons of money selling a tool that made smokers 0.05% less likely to get cancer, would they be heroes or profiteers?

Quant firms would argue that

  • It is not their responsibility to regulate; if the government does not want to protect retail investors, they cannot be blamed
  • They offer everyone a better deal
  • Options are a legitimate product that have real-world uses for managing risk and generating income, beyond their use as speculatory gambling
  • They are simply filling demand from brokers, and if they didn't, someone else would in a less competetive environment at worse prices

All of these are fair and valid pushbacks. You could argue that

  • Something being legal doesn't imply you should interact with it; several options wholesalers have chosen not to market make in prediction markets (for various reasons)
  • Better deals could incentivize more participation
  • While options do have important use cases, retail traders usually go for cheap, short-dated contracts, lose money on average, and join trading frenzies
  • Firms pay billions in PFOF for access to that order flow, so they're not exactly passive in the system, and have lobbied to keep wholesaling as permissive at is

Personally, I think it is

  • A good thing that retail crowds have the ability to participate in derivative markets
  • The regulator's responsibility to make those markets harder to access
  • Important that market makers provide liquidity especially in options markets, where there are ~150 options contracts open at any given time on each underlying stock

I also think that

  • The people who work at these firms are intelligent and know that retail traders will likely lose money on the trades they facilitate
  • These firms pay billions of dollars every year for the opportunity to target these retail traders
  • These firms exist to enrich the people who work at these firms above all else (and are quite open about that fact as well) and that will always trump any moral dilemmas they have in the long run

A bag is a bag, and I can imagine much worse ways to get a bag than wholesaling options. However, just because someone will make money off of providing liquidity to retail traders doesn't mean that you have to be the one raking in the profits.

A disclaimer that I worked on the options desk at Jane Street this summer; they did not like me very much, I did not like them very much, and that probably influences my judgment in some ways. However, my co-interns will tell you I have also been running this debate with full-timers at the firm since the start of the summer, and regardless of how placating their contentions have been, I've never heard a convincing argument of why Jane Street needs to wholesale options beyond the fact that it contributes heavily to their top line.
