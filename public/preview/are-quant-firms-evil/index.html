Are quant firms evil?

What are quant firms, how do they make money, and is it ethical.

Dhruv Gupta

Market makers/wholesalers like Jane Street, Citadel Securities, SIG, and IMC make money by selling assets like stocks or options for marginally more than they're worth, and buying them for marginally less than they're worth (classic buy low sell high).

Every time you try to buy Apple for $5.37 on Robinhood, it's not going to the NASDAQ or NYSE, it's going to a wholesaler, who will sell you that AAPL for $5.38, while the exchanges might have been trading at $5.385.

Not only do these firms do you the great service of giving you a good deal, they also pay Robinhood (or Charles Schwabb) some % for letting them trade against you. Why be so generous?

Quants are terrified of "adverserial selection." The amount of money they make per trade is near 0; their massive profits come from doing millions of trades on both sides of markets per minute.

However, imagine the case of a sell-off. If someone saddles IMC with millions of dollars of a stock minutes before a bad news story causes the price to collapse, IMC will have a very hard time selling off their position without incurring massive losses.

A single trade against smart money (hedge funds, competitors) can wipe out the profits from hunderds of trades against dumb money (most retail investors, institutions like pension funds). Therefore, quant firms are willing to pay a premium to trade against dumb money.

In fact, much of what might be considered "strategies" or "alpha" in market making comes down to understanding if the trader opposite the firm is smart money or dumb money.

Of course, this is a massive oversimplification; the best firms don't simply market make but also are willing to hold positions and take on risk and thus need to have understandings of the fair price of assets, beliefs on how assets will move over short and long time horizons, and novel approaches to trading those assets.

However, if you strip it down to just providing liquidity on exchanges, a money-making heuristic is "does the guy I'm trading against know what he's doing."

Now that we have some understanding of quants, let's chat about options trading.

I won't dive too deeply into the mechanics, but it is important to understand that options trades are negative sum: for every options trade between trader A and trader B, B will lose as much as A gained, and both will give up some % toll to the market makers.

This is opposed to investing in stocks which is positive sum since companies can create net new value, causing their equity to appreciate without any other equity depreciating.

As a consequence of this + the sophistication needed to trade options intelligently, the Indian government found that ~90% of options traders lose money, as opposed to ~5% of stock traders (comparisons over 1 year vs 10 year time horizon and India vs US so take with a grain of salt).

It is concerning, therefore, that ~45% of the volume of options volume in the US is retail, and that the options markets have been growing at a rapider pace than the stock markets.
